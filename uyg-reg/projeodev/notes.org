* Dimensionality Reduction: PLS vs. PCR
** Overview
Both *Principal Component Regression (PCR)* and *Partial Least Squares (PLS)* regression are powerful dimensionality reduction techniques used to handle high-dimensional data and severe multicollinearity.

** Core Differences
The fundamental difference is that PCR is unsupervised, whereas PLS is supervised.

| Feature | Principal Component Regression (PCR) | Partial Least Squares (PLS / PLSR) |
|---------+--------------------------------------+------------------------------------|
| *Approach Type* | *Unsupervised* (ignores Y during feature extraction) | *Supervised* (uses X and Y relationship) |
| *Optimization Goal* | Maximizes *variance* explained strictly within X | Maximizes *covariance* between X and Y |
| *Component Selection* | Chosen because they vary the most in X | Chosen because they are highly predictive of Y |
| *Number of Components* | Generally requires *more components* | Generally requires *fewer components* |
| *Common Use Cases* | General multicollinearity, image/signal denoising | Chemometrics, bioinformatics, spectral data |

** How They Work
*** Principal Component Regression (PCR)
1. Performs Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on predictor matrix X to transform correlated variables into uncorrelated components.
2. Drops lower-variance components and runs standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression on the top components.
3. *Risk:* Can perform poorly if maximum variance directions are unrelated to Y.

*** Partial Least Squares (PLS)
1. Extracts latent components by looking at X and Y simultaneously.
2. Chooses directions along the path of highest correlation between the data matrix and the target.
3. Yields a more compact model with better predictive accuracy using fewer components.

** Shared Characteristics
- *Data Preparation:* Highly sensitive to scale; requires variables to be standardized or normalized first.
- *Interpretability:* Difficult to interpret because final predictors are linear combinations of all original features (no true variable selection).

** Comparative Power
- *Efficiency:* PLSR wins. It extracts maximum target-relevant info per component.
- *Blind Spot Protection:* PLSR wins. PCR may drop critical low-variance predictive signals.
- *Predictive Accuracy:* Tie. When fully optimized with the ideal number of components, maximum predictive accuracies are often identical.

* Robust Regression Methods
** Overview
Designed to *minimize the distorting impact of outliers and noise* in a dataset. Traditional OLS is highly sensitive to extreme points because it minimizes squared residuals.

** Core Mechanisms
1. *Alternative Loss Functions:* Treats large errors differently (e.g., switching from quadratic to linear scaling).
2. *Iteratively Rewweighted Least Squares (IRLS):* Progressively down-weights data points with massive residuals over successive iterations.

** Common Methods
*** Huber Regression (M-Estimation)
- Bridges the gap between OLS and Least Absolute Deviations (LAD).
- Uses squared error for small residuals and absolute error for large residuals based on a threshold ($\delta$).
- *Limitation:* Vulnerable to high-leverage outliers in the predictor variables (X).

*** RANSAC (RANdom SAmple Consensus)
- Non-deterministic algorithm that randomly selects subsets to find the largest group of "inliers".
- Fits the final line exclusively on clean inliers, discarding outliers entirely.

*** Theil-Sen Estimator
- Non-parametric method that computes the slope between every possible pair of points and selects the *median slope*.
- Highly robust but computationally intensive ($O(N^2)$).

*** MM-Estimation
- Combines the high breakdown point of S-estimation with the high efficiency of M-estimation.
- Modern gold standard for general-purpose robust linear modeling.

* Statistical Interpretation & Metrics
** Verbalizing R-Squared ($R^2$)
- *Definition:* The proportion of variance in the dependent variable (Y) explained or predicted by the independent variables (X).
- *Template:* /"Approximately [R² x 100]% of the variation in [Target] can be explained by our model's [Predictors], while the remaining percentage is due to random noise or omitted factors."/
- *Nuances:* It is not "percent accuracy", it does not imply causation, and a "good" value is strictly context-dependent.

** Using P-Values to Justify $R^2$
The $p$-value of the overall F-test measures *statistical reliability*, validating whether your $R^2$ is a real trend or a fluke.

*** Metric Combination Matrix
- *High $R^2$ + Low $p$-value:* Ideal baseline. Highly predictive and statistically bulletproof.
- *Low $R^2$ + Low $p$-value:* Real trend, high noise. Common in human behavior or stock datasets. Trend is structurally sound but subtle.
- *High $R^2$ + High $p$-value:* "Fool's Gold". Indicates overfitting on random noise due to a small sample size or too many variables.
- *Low $R^2$ + High $p$-value:* Failed model. Indistinguishable from a random guess.

** F-Statistic and ANOVA Connection
- *Yes, the F-statistic is the core mathematical engine of ANOVA.*
